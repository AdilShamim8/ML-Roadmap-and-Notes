# Offer Negotiation — Timelines, Leverage, and Scripts

Negotiation is the highest hourly rate you will ever earn: 2–3 emails over a week can move an offer $10–80K. Yet most candidates either accept immediately (leaving the largest, easiest money on the table) or negotiate aggressively with the wrong tools and poison the start. This guide covers how offers actually work, where the leverage is, and the exact scripts — warm, professional, and firm — that don't blow anything up.

**The frame that keeps you sane:** this is a *business conversation between people who want to work together*. The company expects negotiation; a polite ask costs nothing socially at any competent company, and a company that rescinds over a polite ask has done you a favor.

---

## 1. How the Timeline Actually Works

```
final rounds → verbal offer (recruiter call) → written offer
   → your questions + negotiation (3–7 days, ask for it)
   → decision deadline (often 1–2 weeks, sometimes negotiable itself)
   → acceptance → background/start date
```

**The rules of the timeline:**
- **The verbal offer is the opening position.** Almost never the ceiling. Companies budget negotiation room (typically 10–20% for IC roles).
- **Never accept on the verbal call.** Not rudely — script below. The moment you say "yes," the negotiation is over.
- **Get everything in writing** before deciding. Verbal promises ("we'll revisit in 6 months") evaporate; written ones survive.
- **Ask for time properly:** "I'm excited about this — I'd like X days to review the full written offer and make a considered decision." Reasonable asks (2–7 days) are granted; exploding offers ("24 hours") are a red flag worth knowing about *before* you're in them.
- **Competing processes are your clock:** if you have interviews pending, *tell both recruiters* — it legitimately accelerates timelines without any fabrication.

---

## 2. Know the Numbers Before Anyone Says One

Before any recruiter call, research the market bands for **role, level, location (remote/on-site), and company size/stage**:

- Levels.fyi for ML/DS/SWE compensation by company and level (the most accurate public source for tech).
- Glassdoor/Blind for second opinions (noisier; sanity-check outliers).
- The JD's level language: "Senior/Staff/II/III/L4/L5" maps to bands — misreading your level is the most common anchor error.
- For India: Leetcode compensations + Levels.fyi India sections; for EU: levels.fyi + local benchmarks (comp structures differ — US equity-heavy vs EU cash-heavy).

**Compute your three numbers before the call:**
1. **Walk-away floor** — below this, no. (Your current total comp + meaningful bump, or your budget.)
2. **Target** — the realistic strong outcome (top of band for your level, or next-level base).
3. **Aspirational** — the polite moonshot you'll never get but which anchors the conversation (ask once, gracefully).

**Total compensation, always:** base + bonus + equity/year (vested value, not grant-face-value-in-a-pitch) + benefits delta. A "huge" equity grant at a pre-IPO company is a lottery ticket — value it at *your* discount, not theirs.

## 3. Where the Leverage Actually Is

| Leverage | Strength | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Competing written offer | ★★★★★ | The single strongest. Real numbers, real deadline. Never fabricate — this industry is small. |
| Competing process (final rounds) | ★★★★ | "I'm in final rounds with two comparable companies" — legitimate and accelerates. |
| Specific scarce skills | ★★★ | "Your team needs production LLM/RAG experience, which is exactly my last year" — tied to *their* job description. |
| Current comp (if strong) | ★★★ | Works when your current number anchors above their offer; weak if you're underpaid. |
| Counter-offer from current employer | ★★ | Use with care — accepting it keeps you in a job you wanted to leave, and most companies counter to buy transition time, not to keep you forever. |
| Politeness + patience | ★★ | Not leverage per se, but its absence destroys all the above. |

**What is NOT leverage:** how much you *need* the money, your rent, your student loans (never mention need — companies pay for value and scarcity, not sympathy); bluffing (fabricated offers get verified sometimes and always get remembered); aggression (recruiters have long memories and small industries).

## 4. The Scripts

**On the verbal offer call (never accept, never negotiate blind):**

> "Thank you — I'm genuinely excited about this team and the role. I'm going to review the full written details carefully. Could you send the complete package — base, bonus, equity structure, and benefits? Once I've seen everything together, I'll have a couple of questions and I'll be able to give you a considered answer quickly."

**Opening the negotiation (after reading the written offer):**

> "Thank you for the detailed offer — the role is my first choice and I'm ready to sign. One gap: based on my research for [level] in [location] and the scope we discussed — [the specific responsibility that justifies it] — I was expecting total compensation in the range of [target]. Is there flexibility to get there?"

*(Why this works: enthusiastic + ready to sign + a researched, specific ask + a question form. Nothing for the recruiter to be defensive about.)*

**The competing-offer play:**

> "I want to be transparent: I have a written offer from [company] at [number]. Your role is my preference — [specific reason]. If you can match [number] — or come close on total compensation — I'll sign this week."

*(The "I'll sign this week" is what makes a company move: certainty has cash value.)*

**The equity-vs-base trade (when base is capped but you want more):**

> "I understand base is capped for this level. Could we look at an increased equity grant, a signing bonus to bridge year one, or a [6-month] compensation review tied to the responsibilities we discussed? Any of those works for me."

**Asking for time when you need other processes to finish:**

> "I'd like to give this a considered decision rather than a rushed one. Would [date — 5–7 days out] work for my answer? And if it's relevant — I have final rounds at [company] concluding [day]; if those produce an offer, I'd want to weigh them together, and either way you'll hear from me by [date]."

**Handling "is that a hard number?" / pressure tactics:**

> "It's my target based on the market for this level — I'm flexible on the structure, less on the total. What flexibility do you have on your side?"

*(The counter-question turns pressure into information. Recruiters reveal the band's real edges under a soft question more than a hard stance.)*

**The close, either way:**

> Signing: "That works — send the updated offer and I'll sign today. Thank you for working with me on this."
> Declining: "I've decided to go another direction — this was a genuinely difficult call, and I'd love to stay in touch. Thank you for the process."

*(Never burn it: teams reorg, companies re-hire, interviewers move. The declined offer that ends warmly is a network node.)*

## 5. The Mistake Catalog

- **Accepting on the verbal call** — the #1 mistake; ends the conversation at its weakest point for you.
- **Negotiating before the written offer** — you're negotiating against a summary, not a package.
- **Only negotiating base** — base is the least flexible number at most companies; equity/signing/bonus/review-date move more.
- **Revealing your current comp too early** — where legal, deflect: "I'd rather focus on the value for this role — what's the band for the position?" (Note: some jurisdictions — e.g., several US states, and pay-transparency laws elsewhere — either ban the question or require bands on request; know your local rules.)
- **Multiple renegotiations** — one well-structured round, maybe a small follow-up. A third round signals bad-faith and burns start-date goodwill.
- **Fabricating competing offers** — occasionally verified, always remembered, sometimes disqualifying. Real processes only.
- **Ignoring the non-compete/IP clauses** — read them *before* negotiating comp; a non-compete is a compensation issue wearing legal clothes. Have an employment lawyer glance at anything nonstandard (an hour of legal review is cheap insurance on a six-figure commitment).
- **Neglecting the start date** — it's negotiable too; if you need a break between jobs (you do), ask for it now, politely.

## 6. Evaluating the Final Offer (the 30-Minute Spreadsheet)

Before the final yes, one honest hour: list every component of every offer (and your current job) — base, bonus target/realized, equity per year (at *your* valuation of the risk), 401k/retirement match, insurance premium delta, learning budget, PTO, commute/remote policy — then three non-money rows: *growth* (will this role compound my skills?), *team* (did I like the interviewers? would I learn from them?), *optionality* (does this role open more doors than it closes?).

Money is the easiest dimension to compare and rarely the most important one over a 3-year horizon. The standard heuristic: **take the money seriously for one honest hour, then weigh growth/team/optionality at full weight** — almost nobody's career regret is "I took the role with the better manager and steeper learning curve for 8% less money."

**The last check — the "Monday feeling" test:** picture yourself on a random Monday in six months in this role, at this company, with these people. Whatever your gut does in that picture is the most data-dense signal you'll get. Trust it over the spreadsheet's marginal row.
